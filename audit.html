How to verify

Recompute the root digest

Given a copy of the source under NDA, this reproduces the digest below. If it differs, you were not given the code this site describes, and that is worth knowing before you read anything else on it.

find service opsco -name '*.py' ! -name '__init__.py' \
  | sort | xargs sha256sum -t | sha256sum

# -t is text mode. Binary mode writes ' *path' instead of two spaces and
# yields a different digest. On macOS: xargs shasum -a 256 | shasum -a 256

opsco/attest.py 159 lines · 6 public · e9a1914ef091…

Hash-chained, Ed25519-signed attestation log.

This is where the non-repudiation you actually want lives. Each record commits to its predecessor, so no entry can be altered or removed without breaking every link after it; each is signed by a named key, so the issuer cannot later deny having issued it. Anchor the head hash daily somewhere outside OpsCo's control (a timestamping authority, or a counter-signed record held by the partner org) and the chain also cannot be wholesale replaced.

opsco/lexicon.py 277 lines · 10 public · 296f5114410a…

The ink economy: documents as instruments, and words priced by what they cost to write.

Ink in this system is not a supply. It is *minted against an attested event* and it is spent, which gives it the two properties money needs and a stationery cupboard does not: it cannot be conjured, and every millilitre traces to a lot, a purchase order and a named supplier. A document that spends ink is therefore a claim on stock somebody physically bought, and a claim that can be checked twice — against the ledger that issued it, and against the residue it left.

opsco/spool.py 297 lines · 12 public · c3e1fa30727a…

Adversarial glyphs: marks that wear the device, and marks the machine misreads.

The two failures in this module are the same failure seen from opposite ends. A glyph is a shape that a person reads and a machine reads, and those two readings can be pulled apart. Pull them apart one way and the device remembers a mark long after the job — that is **burn-in**. Pull them apart the other way and the page says one thing to a reader and another to whatever is watching the spool — that is a **transcription split**.
